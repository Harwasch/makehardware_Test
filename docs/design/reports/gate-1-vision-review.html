How does the Mako dock?

Three docking arrangements, drawn from real geometry. This is the agreement gate that was skipped — the concepts were built, never shown, and everything downstream has been assuming a vision nobody signed off.

What I need from you: pick one, or tell me the constraint I've missed.

The docking arrangement sets coil orientation, what alignment the link can count on, whether the pads can be serviced, and whether anything has to actuate. Everything in the electrical design downstream rests on it. There is also a second decision below, on the coil itself, where I got it wrong the first time and have corrected it.

Three ways to dock

pick one
A

Belly dock

simplest 1700 × 1110 × 560 mm

The Mako sinks onto a horizontal cradle and its own weight holds it down.

Both pads horizontal and facing up — RX flush in the belly, TX in the cradle floor. Gravity does the clamping, so there is no actuator and nothing to fail closed. Two vee-guides funnel the hull as it descends. The 4 × 8 inch rectangle lies along the hull where it fits naturally.

Argues for
  • No actuator — gravity clamps
  • Rectangle suits the hull's long axis
  • Fewest moving parts of the three
Argues against
  • Pads face up, so silt and debris settle on them
  • Servicing means lifting the vehicle off
  • Gap set by hull sag, not by a mechanism
B

Flank dock

serviceable 1700 × 656 × 916 mm

The Mako holds station alongside a vertical face and a light clamp pulls it in.

Both pads vertical, the RX pad let into the flank. Nothing settles on a vertical surface, and both pads stay reachable without lifting anything. The cost is an active clamp to hold the gap, and the vehicle has to hold attitude through the approach.

Argues for
  • Pads shed silt — nothing settles on vertical
  • Both pads serviceable in place
  • Clamp sets the gap positively
Argues against
  • Needs an active clamp and its failure mode
  • Vehicle must hold attitude while docking
  • Most misalignment of the three
C

Saddle dock

best alignment 1700 × 1080 × 733 mm

The hull drops into a 90° vee and self-centres on two contact lines.

A pad in each face of the vee, so the vehicle carries two RX coils at ±45° on the lower flanks and the link splits across both. Alignment is the best of the three and needs no actuator — the geometry does it. The cost is doubling the coil count and combining two links at the receiver.

Argues for
  • Self-centring — best alignment by geometry
  • No actuator
  • Two smaller links share the heat
Argues against
  • Doubles the coil count and the BOM
  • Receiver must combine two links
  • Vee traps silt at the bottom

The coil, corrected

I got this wrong · now fixed

What I told you before was wrong

I reported that an etched PCB coil could not meet the loss budget and that Litz was required. That conclusion rested on a single-layer analysis which I then generalised to "the etched spiral" as a category. Parallel layers are the obvious lever and I never modelled them. With the correction, a PCB coil clears the requirement with 1.57× margin.

Recomputed for your 4 × 8 inch rectangle — Grover's rectangular-loop formula per turn, numerical Neumann integration for every turn pair and for the coil-to-coil mutual, so gap, offset and tilt are all handled without a symmetry assumption.

Why fine traces help

Driving the trace to 0.80 mm almost eliminates proximity effect — the AC/DC resistance ratio falls from 1.77 at a 3 mm trace to 1.06 — because the critical frequency scales as (w+s)/w². The coil stops being a proximity problem and becomes a pure DC-resistance problem, which is exactly what parallel layers fix. Two counterintuitive corollaries: past 4 oz, more copper makes it worse, and a bigger coil makes it worse because it needs wider traces.

Why six layers, not four

The layer model assumes ideal 1/N resistance scaling. Real stacked spirals do worse, because each layer sits in the others' field — and a turn-to-turn model cannot capture that. Four layers passes with only 1.10× margin, which is not enough to also absorb ferrite and cold-plate losses that are not modelled at all. Inter-layer proximity is now the single largest uncertainty in the design and is the first thing FEA should answer.

The rectangle costs coupling, and the thermal problem got harder. k falls from 0.705 on a 300 mm circular pad to 0.437 worst-case here, because a 20 mm gap is 20% of the short dimension rather than 7% of a diameter. And 38 W leaving a 102 × 203 mm pad is 1858 W/m² against 764 W/m² for the circular pad — 3.6× less area for the same watts. MEC-003's 0.40 coupling floor now has only 1.09× margin, so if FEA shows the cold plate costing more than ~8% of coupling, either that floor or the coil dimensions have to move.

Open questions

answers change the design
  • What does the Mako actually look like? The hull in every render above is a placeholder — a 1.4 m capsule, drawn so the pad has something believable to sit on. Its real form factor drives where a 4 × 8 inch rectangle can go and how well it aligns.
  • Is the 1:2 aspect ratio fixed? It is what forces six layers rather than four — a squarer pad of the same area couples better. If 4 × 8 inches is a hard envelope constraint, fine; if it is a convenience, a 5 × 6.5 inch pad would buy margin back.
  • Does the Mako charge in the water, or is it recovered to deck first? In the water, the RX pad has an essentially infinite heat sink and the two pads should not share a symmetric loss budget at all. On deck at +55 °C, they should.
  • Should the in-band link authenticate? A dock that delivers 3 kW to anything answering correctly is worth a deliberate decision. My default is to reserve a protocol field and not implement it.
